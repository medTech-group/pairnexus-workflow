# Transcript 4: a salon owner who has run the diary by phone for twenty years

*Simulated.* The answers of a made-up persona (`briefs/4.md`, role: the skeptic), given by a `research-persona` agent from its brief and the questions alone, word for word.

Q1: The last one I remember was a regular at the till after her colour last week. I said "same again in six weeks?", opened the book, saw that Thursday mornings were full and offered her the Friday at ten. I pencilled her name in with a "T" for tint and wrote her number in. That took about a minute while she found her purse. She never "thought she needed a haircut". Booking the next visit is just part of leaving. (Details of this particular booking are my guess, but the habit is in my brief.)

Q2: Mostly they stay with whoever cut them first, and I'm the one who decides who that is. When a new client rings, I listen to what they want. A tricky colour comes to me. A quick trim might go to my part-timer. The girl who rents the chair keeps her own clients, and that's her business. If someone's stylist isn't free, I'll tell them straight and offer another day or another pair of hands. My guess: most of them would rather wait than switch.

Q3: If I've got scissors in my hand, it rings out or someone at the backwash picks up. Regulars call back, or they drop in, because they live round the corner. I'll check missed numbers between clients and ring back the ones I don't recognise. I don't believe the "lost calls" figures. They come from the people selling the answering machine, and the same people say most clients still prefer to phone. Do I lose some? My guess is a few, but I've never counted, and nobody selling me something is going to count them fairly.

Q4: Most bookings happen at the till, straight after an appointment. The phone is busiest first thing in the morning and around lunchtime, and before Christmas and wedding season it doesn't stop. My guess: the people ringing in the evenings are newcomers. My regulars know when I'm open.

Q5: Someone rings or pops in, I rub out the pencil and either fill the gap or leave it for a walk-in. Last time it happened, the client rang that morning because her child was ill. I moved her to the next week and gave the slot to a walk-in. With pencil and a rubber it's very quick. What I don't want is people moving themselves around in my day without me knowing why.

Q6: Mostly they don't get a reminder. Regulars know, and some write it on the little card I give them. For a long colour, or a new client, I might ring the day before. When someone forgets, I lose the slot, and with tight margins I feel it. But I'm not going to charge a twenty-year client for forgetting once.

Q7: For me, they'd need to ring me. That's the safe way, because I know their voice. If anyone can cancel with a link and no account, what stops someone cancelling someone else's booking, or a client cancelling at eight in the morning without me seeing it until the empty chair? Safe means I know about it and can do something with the slot. Unsafe means it happens behind my back.

Q8: The book sits on the desk. I deal with bookings between clients, at the till, and sometimes with the phone tucked under my chin while I'm cutting. It costs me a bit of attention, but I'm being paid for that client while I talk. Last time we had a system, it lived on a tablet nobody looked at. The subscription kept going up and the girls stopped using it, so we went back to paper.

Q9: I need to know who it is, what they're having and who's doing it. Then I decide how long it takes, and that's the bit software gets wrong. "Full head colour" takes one time for one client and half as long again for another with long thick hair. Price matters to the client, so I tell them a rough figure on the phone. I also want to know who's new, because new clients get a consultation and a longer slot.

Q10: I need to see it in the book, on the day page, before the day starts. That's where I see the gaps, who's in when, and where I'm keeping room for walk-ins. If a booking turns up somewhere else, like on my phone or in an email, it might as well not exist. My guess: I'd want to approve each booking before it counts, which probably defeats the point of these tools.

Q11: My guess, because I don't have exact cases: a couple of no-shows on long colour appointments cost me the best part of a morning's takings. Once in a while I've written something in pencil that I couldn't read later. The worst time in recent years was the software, though. I paid for months for something nobody used, spent time on setup, and confused clients who'd been sent links they didn't want.

Q12: I'd look at whether the book is fuller and whether I'm earning more after paying for the thing. I'd ask whether the girls still use it after three months without me nagging, whether my older regulars complain, and whether I'm keeping gaps for walk-ins or the system has filled them with the wrong things. I'd also watch whether the price has gone up. If I'm spending more time sorting out the system than I spent on the phone, it's worse, whatever the dashboard says.
